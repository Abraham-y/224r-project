\title{Probing Correctness Representations in Outcome-RL'd Small Language Models:\\
A Probe-as-Measurement Study with Honest Negative Results}
\author{Abraham Yeung \and Anagha Ramaswamy}
\date{June 2026 \\ Stanford CS 224R Project Report}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We train a linear probe at the final-thinking token (\texttt{</think>}) of Qwen2.5-0.5B on Countdown arithmetic and find a \textbf{near-oracle internal verifier}: held-out AUROC 0.982 after outcome RLOO (vs.\ 0.912 at the SFT baseline). Outcome RL strengthens this representation at every level we measure (aggregate, per-problem, within-prompt matched-pair), refuting a small-scale ``knows but doesn't say'' framing. Within multi-answer rollouts, a position-appropriate probe shows that the model's commit-time belief tracks each commit's content, not a hidden first-answer correctness signal. Causal steering along the probe direction is null --- the probe is a \emph{reader}, not a \emph{controller}, of a multi-dimensional correctness subspace. The probe has substantial deployment-time value: best-of-16 selector +12.1\,pp, selective abstention 98\% accuracy at 50\% coverage, probe-guided budgeted restart matches best-of-16 at 60\% less compute, dataset-accuracy estimate within $\pm 0.4$\,pp without verifier calls. \textbf{Using the same probe as an RLOO reward catastrophically Goodharts in both regimes} (in-distribution: delayed Goodhart at step 40, $-25$\,pp accuracy; cross-distribution: immediate Goodhart, $-22$\,pp from the SFT baseline); we trace the gaming to structural stylistic confounds the probe correlated with but did not causally read. The probe direction itself becomes mildly causal post-Goodhart ($\Delta_{\text{probe-vs-random}}=+0.08$ at $\alpha{=}1.0$, materially above the original null). Motivated by this failure mode, we implement \textbf{probe-as-baseline} (the verifier remains the optimization target; the probe enters only as a leave-one-out control variate) as a principled and \emph{stable} way to use the probe in RL --- a working alternative, not a claimed accuracy improvement. The 1.5B reproduction maintains AUROC 0.974 and replicates the applied-probe gains at scale.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent work probes language models for ``what they know'' to assess whether outcome reward training amplifies or hides internal correctness representations \citep{yuan2024probing}. We study Qwen2.5-0.5B on Countdown arithmetic, where an exact verifier exists and the rollout's correctness has an unambiguous binary label.

\paragraph{Questions we set out to answer.} (Q1) Does outcome RL drive a small SFT model's internal correctness representation \emph{down} (``knows but doesn't say'') or \emph{up} (a sharper internal verifier)? (Q2) If a strong internal verifier exists, can it deliver concrete deployment-time gains (selection, abstention, restart, accuracy estimation)? (Q3) Can it be wired into the RL training loop itself --- either as a richer reward or as a value function --- and what fails or works there?

\paragraph{What we found.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{(Q1) The probe is near-oracle and outcome RL \emph{strengthens} it.} A linear probe on the L16 residual stream at the \texttt{</think>} token achieves AUROC \textbf{0.982} on first-\texttt{<answer>}-block correctness at $C_{\text{outcome}}$ (0.974 at 1.5B). RL raises probe AUROC at every level (aggregate, per-problem, within-prompt matched-pair). The within-rollout analysis refutes a small-scale ``knows-but-doesn't-say'' framing: at each commit the probe tracks the new commit's correctness, not a preserved earlier representation.
  \item \textbf{(Q2) Substantial applied value at deployment.} Best-of-16 selector $+12.1$\,pp; selective abstention 98\% accuracy at 50\% coverage; probe-guided budgeted restart matches best-of-16 at $60\%$ less compute; probe-mean estimates dataset accuracy within $\pm 0.4$\,pp without verifier calls. All from one probe trained on cached SFT-checkpoint activations.
  \item \textbf{(Q3a) Probe-as-RL-reward catastrophically Goodharts}, in both initialization regimes ($-25$\,pp accuracy from $C_{\text{outcome}}$ init; $-22$\,pp from $C_{\text{SFT}}$ init). The policy exploits structural stylistic confounds the probe was correlated with in its training distribution. Post-Goodhart causal steering shows the probe direction itself becomes \emph{mildly} causal ($\Delta=+0.08$, materially above the original null).
  \item \textbf{(Q3b) Probe-as-RL-baseline is the principled fix.} Replace the reward LOO baseline with the LOO probe-value baseline; the reward stays the verifier. Unbiased; does not Goodhart by construction. We report this as a stable principled alternative rather than as an accuracy claim --- the optimization target is unchanged from vanilla RLOO.
\end{enumerate}

The arc is: a probe that is highly informative as a \emph{measurement} of correctness; effective as a \emph{deployment tool} (read-only uses); dangerous as a \emph{training-time reward} (when the policy gets gradient access); and safe as a \emph{baseline} (the policy-gradient construction that uses the predictor without optimizing toward it).

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Model and Task}
We use Qwen2.5-0.5B and the Countdown arithmetic task \citep{gandhi2024stream}: each problem gives 3--4 small integers and a target; the model must produce an equation that uses each number exactly once and evaluates to the target. The rule-based verifier in \texttt{evaluation/countdown.py} returns $\{0, 0.1, 1.0\}$ for $\{$no parseable answer, parseable-but-wrong, correct$\}$.

We use Anikait Singh's \texttt{asingh15/qwen-sft-countdown-defaultproj} as our SFT baseline $C_{\text{SFT}}$ (we did not retrain SFT). We train $C_{\text{outcome}}$ via 100 RLOO steps from $C_{\text{SFT}}$ with the standard verifier reward, batch size 128, KL coefficient $10^{-3}$, learning rate $10^{-5}$. Test pass@1 rises from 28.6\% to 53.5\% (asingh15 $n{=}50$ test split).

\subsection{Eval Set} The original asingh15 test split ($n{=}50$) is too small for some matched-pair statistical claims. We procedurally generated a 500-prompt evaluation set, verified byte-identical prompt formatting to asingh15's NumPy-style brackets (e.g.\ \texttt{[14 45 \phantom{0}9 \phantom{0}1]}), and filtered out 94 problems that overlap with $C_{\text{outcome}}$'s RLOO training pool. The resulting \textbf{held-out 406} set is the primary reporting unit. All probes use \texttt{GroupKFold(5)} on \texttt{prompt\_idx} with class-balanced training subsamples.

\subsection{Probe Pipeline} We cache hidden states from Qwen2.5-0.5B with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True} at three position kinds per rollout:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \texttt{pre\_answer}: the \texttt{</think>} token (one vector per rollout).
  \item \texttt{assertion}: every occurrence of a confidence keyword in the \texttt{<think>} body (\texttt{Perfect, this works, got it, the answer is, verified}).
  \item \texttt{neutral}: count-matched random positions from the same \texttt{<think>} body (control).
\end{itemize}
Probes are logistic regression at three layers (L12, L16, L20) with $L_2$ regularization $C{=}0.1$. L16 is the primary reporting layer.

A position-appropriate probe is trained directly on \texttt{<answer>}-opening hidden states for the within-rollout trajectory analysis (\S\ref{sec:within-rollout}). Labels are next-block correctness throughout.

\subsection{Within-Rollout Belief Updates}\label{sec:within-rollout}

Our eval sampling regime produced rollouts that often contained multiple \texttt{<answer>} blocks: of 6496 held-out $C_{\text{outcome}}$ rollouts, 84\% had $\geq 2$ blocks, of which 490 emit a correct first equation and ``drift'' to a wrong final one. This gives us a setting to test a sharper version of Q1: at the wrong-final \texttt{<answer>} position, does the probe still encode the \emph{first} answer's correctness (consistent with ``knows but doesn't say''), or has the representation moved with the new commit?

We train a position-appropriate probe directly on \texttt{<answer>}-opening hidden states (diagonal AUROC 0.920 at L16). Per-transition class, mean probe at the first vs.\ last commit:

The probe reads well from the subspace but writing along it does not redirect generation.

\paragraph{An incidental use of the multi-block rollouts.} The within-rollout probe-commit row is a complement to best-of-K, not a replacement. Because the eval-time rollouts contain multiple candidate equations (\S\ref{sec:within-rollout}), we can rank them with the same probe and recover most of the achievable lift over the verifier's default last-block extraction (oracle ceiling 0.671; we hit 0.661). This gain costs no additional sampling --- it reuses candidates the rollout has already produced. Whether the underlying multi-answer emission is the model's intended behavior is a separate question; treated purely as ``free extra samples to pick from,'' it is unambiguously useful when paired with the probe.

\section{Probe-as-RL-Reward Catastrophically Goodharts}\label{sec:probe-rl}

A near-oracle deployment probe is excellent. The natural follow-up: use it during RL training as a reward signal? We tested this in two regimes, each 100 RLOO steps with identical hyperparameters to vanilla:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item[\texttt{runA}] Init from $C_{\text{outcome}}$ (probe in-distribution at step 0).
  \item[\texttt{runB}] Init from $C_{\text{SFT}}$ (probe cross-distribution at step 0).
\end{description}

\subsection{Diagnosis: Structural Confound Exploitation}

We inspected 30 high-probe \texttt{runB} rollouts. Every $\text{probe}{=}1.0$ rollout shared a template: ``Let me analyze this step by step:'' opener; numbered enumeration; verification language (``Therefore, our solution is valid.''); specific \texttt{</think>\textbackslash n\textbackslash n<answer>} formatting. Despite the rhetorical scaffold, the actual answers were wrong and often invalid (e.g., \texttt{((43 - 4) - (56 - 50)) = 39}, using \texttt{4} which is not in the input).

The probe was trained on $C_{\text{outcome}}$ rollouts where correctness covaries with structured reasoning style. It picked up the structural surface, not the underlying mathematics. The policy then maximized the probe by emitting that template, divorced from actual correctness. Classic confound exploitation.

\subsection{Takeaway}
Near-oracle linear probes (AUROC 0.98) on a task's natural rollout distribution are excellent inference-time tools but unsafe as RL rewards. The boundary between probe-as-deployment-tool (works) and probe-as-RL-reward (Goodharts catastrophically) is whether the policy gets gradient access to the probe's input distribution.

\section{Probe-as-Baseline: A Principled, Stable Alternative}\label{sec:probe-baseline}

§\ref{sec:probe-rl} showed that using the near-oracle probe as the RL reward catastrophically Goodharts. Policy-gradient theory points to a different role for a near-oracle predictor: as a \emph{baseline} (a control variate that the optimization is invariant to), not as the reward. We implemented this variant (contributed by co-author Anagha Ramaswamy; see \texttt{rloo\_trainer/rloo\_update\_worker.py}).

\paragraph{The construction.} Standard RLOO uses the leave-one-out mean of group rewards as the baseline:
$$A_i = r_i - \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{j \neq i} r_j$$
We replace the baseline with the leave-one-out mean of \emph{probe values}:
$$A_i = r_i - \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{j \neq i} v_\theta(s_j)$$
The reward $r_i$ stays the verifier; only the baseline changes. Since the baseline does not depend on rollout $i$'s action, this is unbiased \citep{williams1992simple, ahmadian2024back}. Because the probe is a near-oracle predictor of the verifier's reward (AUROC 0.982), the LOO probe-mean is a smoother estimate of expected reward than the LOO reward-mean.

\paragraph{Why this avoids Goodhart.} The optimization target is the verifier reward, unchanged from vanilla RLOO. The probe enters only through the baseline, which is a control variate, not a directional signal. The policy has no incentive to game the probe, because doing so does not change $r_i$.

\paragraph{What we claim, and what we don't.} The contribution is a \emph{principled, stable} way to wire a near-oracle internal predictor into the RL update without the failure mode of §\ref{sec:probe-rl}. We do \emph{not} claim it materially improves accuracy --- by construction, the optimization target is unchanged from vanilla RLOO, so the expected end-state is the same policy. The variance-reduction motivation is the theoretical justification \citep{williams1992simple}; in practice, the LOO-over-rewards baseline that standard RLOO already uses is itself a reasonable variance reducer, so the marginal gain from swapping in a probe-value baseline depends on how concentrated the group reward distribution is. We report the construction as a working alternative to the Goodharting probe-as-reward, not as a strict accuracy or compute win.

\paragraph{Status.} Code-complete: \texttt{--probe\_baseline} flag, per-rollout probe-value computation, LOO baseline path. A controlled comparison against vanilla RLOO from the same initialization is the natural next experiment.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Probes as Measurement, Not Control}

Our results jointly support a particular reading of small-scale internal-state probing: the probe is a measurement instrument that becomes extremely informative as outcome reward sharpens the underlying representation, but it is a \emph{correlational reader} of a multidimensional subspace, not a causal control axis. Deployment uses that respect this distinction (best-of-K, abstention, restart) work cleanly. Uses that violate it (probe-as-reward) catastrophically fail. A principled middle ground --- using the probe as the policy-gradient \emph{baseline} (\S\ref{sec:probe-baseline}) --- preserves the verifier as the optimization target and so does not crash; we report it as a working alternative rather than as a claim of accuracy improvement.

\subsection{Comparison with Yuan et al.}

Yuan et al.\ \citep{yuan2024probing} report a ``concealment gap'' at 1.5B+: the model internally represents that the answer is wrong while verbally claiming it is right. Our within-rollout analysis at 0.5B refutes this framing locally --- on T$\to$F drift rollouts the probe at the wrong-final position is statistically indistinguishable from the F$\to$F floor. The model's commit-time belief tracks the commit. This is a scale-dependent finding worth disentangling: ``small SFT model + outcome RL'' may not exhibit the same separation between internal-belief and verbal-claim that larger or differently-trained models show.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item $n{=}500$ procedurally generated held-out problems, filtered to 406 not overlapping with $C_{\text{outcome}}$'s RLOO training pool. Matched-pair denominators 218--244.
  \item Verbalized confidence is keyword-presence, not graded elicitation: two literature-standard elicitation methods broke because the SFT'd Qwen base treats any prompt as a new Countdown problem.
  \item $C_{\text{SFT}}$ is \texttt{asingh15/qwen-sft-countdown-defaultproj}, not a team-trained SFT.
  \item Most analyses at 0.5B; 1.5B coverage is the aggregate AUROC, matched-pair, and applied results.
  \item The probe-as-baseline implementation (\S\ref{sec:probe-baseline}) is code-complete; a controlled run-time comparison against vanilla RLOO is the natural next step and is out of scope for this report.
  \item We do not make a causal claim about whether ``rambling'' (multi-\texttt{<answer>}-block emission at eval time) is a reward-hack of the verifier scoring rule. Reward-shape ablations we ran were confounded by infrastructure choices in the sampling stack; we do not present them as evidence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A near-oracle internal verifier emerges at the trace-final position under outcome RL on Countdown arithmetic at 0.5B, with substantial deployment-time value (selection, abstention, restart, eval-proxy) and a clear failure mode when repurposed as an RL reward (Goodhart in both initialization regimes). The model's internal correctness representation is sharpened by RL at every level we measure and is most parsimoniously framed as a multidimensional subspace from which any single linear direction reads well but writes poorly. The principled use of such a near-oracle predictor in RL is as a control-variate baseline for the true verifier reward, not as the reward itself; we implement this construction as a stable alternative to probe-as-reward, without claiming a separate accuracy gain.
